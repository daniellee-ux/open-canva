# Write marketing copy

Convert the user's source into a content package that earns attention, keeps its promise, and is easy to lay out. Treat copy as a sequence—discovery, click, read-through, trust, then action—not as one undifferentiated paragraph.

## Protect the promise

- Preserve the source's facts, qualifications, and causal limits.
- Never invent results, testimonials, credentials, urgency, scarcity, or authority.
- Mark missing proof as a question or placeholder; do not silently fill it.
- Make the title and cover compelling by selecting a sharp truthful angle, not by overstating the content.
- Ensure every promise made before the click is paid off early in the body.

## Build the brief

Extract or infer these fields before writing:

- **Audience:** who should immediately recognize themselves?
- **Current state:** what are they trying, feeling, fearing, or misunderstanding?
- **Desired change:** what useful outcome can this content honestly deliver?
- **Source truth:** which facts, examples, stories, and claims may be used?
- **Goal:** click, read, save, share, reply, sign up, or buy?
- **Channel:** Xiaohongshu, Instagram, LinkedIn, ad, landing graphic, or a generic social post?
- **Voice:** the user's natural tone, brand voice, or supplied reference samples.

Ask only for a missing input that would materially change the result. Otherwise state a concise assumption and proceed. When the target is Xiaohongshu, read [references/xiaohongshu.md](references/xiaohongshu.md).

## Choose one content angle

Express the angle as:

> For **[audience in a recognizable situation]**, show **[specific useful change]** through **[credible mechanism or proof]**, so they can **[action or identity payoff]**.

Prefer an angle that scores well on:

1. **Relevance** — the intended reader recognizes “this is for me.”
2. **Specificity** — the situation and payoff are concrete.
3. **Tension** — there is a gap, surprise, mistake, tradeoff, or unresolved question.
4. **Credibility** — the source contains enough proof to support it.
5. **Share motive** — sharing helps someone, signals identity, or gives language to a felt experience.

Do not combine several weak promises. Pick one dominant promise and make the rest supporting points.

## Write the discovery package

Draft three meaningfully different candidates, then select one. Vary the mechanism, not just synonyms:

- **Benefit:** state what the reader can gain.
- **Pain:** name a familiar costly problem.
- **Relief:** offer a simpler, faster, or less painful route without false ease.
- **Recognition:** articulate a feeling or situation the audience has not named clearly.
- **Contrarian:** challenge a familiar belief only when the body can prove the reversal.
- **Authority:** borrow a study, expert, or institution only when the source is verifiable and accurately represented.
- **Emotion:** lead with delight, frustration, fear, hope, or surprise without manipulating or shaming.

For each candidate, write:

- **Title:** carries topic, searchable language, and the main frame.
- **Cover copy:** creates immediate self-relevance and a reason to click.
- **Hook:** pays off the promise in the first lines instead of repeating the title.

Do not make title and cover copy identical. Let them add information to each other while pointing to the same promise.

## Structure for read-through

Use the shortest structure that fully delivers the value:

1. **Hook:** recognition, outcome, tension, or a concrete scene.
2. **Orientation:** tell the reader what they will get and why this source is worth their time.
3. **Value units:** one idea per paragraph, card, or carousel board; front-load the useful sentence.
4. **Proof:** example, demonstration, comparison, result, source, or clearly labeled personal experience.
5. **Payoff:** restate the change in practical terms.
6. **CTA:** request one natural next action that follows from the content.

For a story, use **setup → obstacle → turn → change → transferable takeaway**. Include only details that increase understanding, emotion, or credibility.

## Make the copy easy to read

- Use short sentences and one idea per block.
- Replace abstractions with observable situations, actions, objects, and numbers from the source.
- Put the conclusion before the explanation when the reader needs the answer first.
- Use headings and lists as navigation, not decoration.
- Remove throat-clearing, duplicate claims, generic motivation, and empty superlatives.
- Preserve useful nuance, but move caveats next to the claim they qualify.
- Match the audience's language; prefer the words they search or say over industry jargon.

## Keep a human voice

When samples exist, extract recurring traits—sentence length, rhythm, vocabulary, stance, humor, and favorite transitions—then write with those constraints. Never imitate a living creator's distinctive style on request; use high-level traits to create an original voice.

After drafting:

- Replace stock AI transitions and perfectly symmetrical phrasing.
- Add only source-grounded observations, minor texture, and natural rhythm.
- Read aloud and simplify anything a person would not naturally say.
- Keep deliberate roughness subtle; clarity matters more than forced slang.

## Final audit

Confirm all of the following:

- The intended reader can recognize relevance in two seconds.
- The title and cover make one clear, supported promise.
- The opening pays off that promise immediately.
- Each section advances the reader instead of restating the premise.
- Facts and proof trace back to the user's source.
- The CTA asks for one proportionate action.
- A reader has a concrete reason to save or share it.
